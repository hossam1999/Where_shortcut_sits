\keybox{\textbf{Summary.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Medical image classifiers often learn \emph{artifacts} (rulers, hair, calipers, debris) instead of the disease.
The usual defence is \emph{ROI masking}: keep only the lesion or organ and delete the rest of the image.
\item We found that masking works only when the artifact lies \emph{outside} the region of interest (ROI). When the
artifact lies \emph{inside} the ROI, masking keeps it, removes everything else, and the model relies on the artifact
\emph{even more}.
\item We showed this in four steps: (1) moving a ruler into a skin lesion step by step; (2) four checks of
\emph{why} it happens; (3) the same test in four other kinds of medical images; (4) real, unedited artifacts in four
patient cohorts.
\item With real artifacts, masking was worth much more when the artifact was outside the ROI than when it was inside, in
all four cohorts (all four tests significant after correction for multiple testing).
\end{itemize}}

\section{The problem}
A deep classifier learns whatever predicts the label in its training data, including things that are not the disease
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. This is called a \emph{shortcut}. In medical images the shortcut is often an artifact
that clinicians put there because they suspected disease: a ruler or skin marking next to a suspicious mole
\citep{winkler2019markings,winkler2021scalebars}, calipers on a thyroid nodule that was measured, or a chest drain in a
patient with a collapsed lung \citep{jimenez2023shortcuts}. Because these artifacts appear more often in sick patients,
a model can score well by detecting the artifact. It then fails on exactly the patients whose images look different,
for example a melanoma photographed without a ruler.

The most common defence is to segment the lesion and give the model only that region; the rest of the image is
deleted. This is called \emph{region-of-interest (ROI) masking}. It is popular because it needs only a lesion mask,
which many pipelines already compute. Its hidden assumption is that the artifact lies in the background. Rulers,
hair, calipers and debris often lie on or across the lesion. Figure~\ref{fig:idea} shows what masking then does.

\begin{figure}[!htb]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/masking_idea.pdf}
\caption{The idea. Top: an artifact outside the lesion is deleted by the mask. Bottom: an artifact on the lesion
survives the mask, while everything that competed with it is deleted.}\label{fig:idea}\end{figure}

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{Does the benefit of ROI masking depend on where the artifact sits relative to the
ROI? If so, why, and does it hold for real artifacts and other kinds of medical images?}

\section{How we test it}
\paragraph{Two models} Every experiment compares two models that use the same frozen image encoder (a pretrained
foundation model such as DINOv2 \citep{oquab2024dinov2}) and the same simple classifier on top:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item the \textbf{standard model} sees the whole image;
\item the \textbf{masked model} sees the image with everything outside the ROI replaced by a plain grey value, both
in training and in testing.
\end{itemize}
We keep the encoder frozen so that the only difference between the two models is the input.

\paragraph{The trap} To measure how much a model relies on the artifact, we build a ``trap''
(Figure~\ref{fig:env}). In the training data the artifact appears on 90\% of the diseased images and 10\% of the
healthy ones, so it is a strong shortcut. We then test on three test sets:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{correlated test}: same 90/10 pattern as in training. A shortcut model looks good here.
\item \textbf{reversed test}: the pattern is flipped (10/90). A model that uses the artifact \emph{fails} here.
\item \textbf{clean test}: the artifact is unrelated to the disease. This shows how well the model knows the disease.
\end{itemize}
\begin{figure}[!htb]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{figures/environments.pdf}
\caption{The trap. Bars show how often the artifact appears among diseased (red) and healthy (grey) images in
training and in each test set.}\label{fig:env}\end{figure}

\paragraph{The score} We report AUROC (0.5 = guessing, 1.0 = perfect). Our main score is the AUROC on the
\emph{reversed} test: it is high only if the model looks at the disease and not at the artifact. We usually report the
\emph{difference} ``masked minus standard''. A positive number means masking helped; a negative number means it hurt.
In square brackets we give the 95\% confidence interval; if it does not include zero, the difference is significant.

\paragraph{Measuring fairly} Two details turned out to matter.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Near-duplicate images.} Skin-lesion datasets contain several photos of the same lesion. If one copy is in
training and another in testing, the test partly measures memory. We always split by lesion (and by image similarity),
never by single images. With random splits, 93 near-duplicate pairs crossed the split and the measured shortcut was
inflated by 10--18\% (Table~\ref{tab:leak}).
\item \textbf{Honest error bars.} We train each model several times (different random seeds), but all runs are tested
on the \emph{same} test images. A common way of computing confidence intervals treats these runs as if each had its own
test set, which makes the intervals too narrow. We resample the seeds and the test images together, so the test set
counts only once. Our intervals are about 1.5 times wider than the common method gives, and a few results that looked
significant with the common method are not.
\end{itemize}
Before each main experiment we wrote down the design and what result would count as support, and stored it in the
project repository; results are reported whichever way they went.

\section{Step 1: moving a ruler into the lesion}\label{sec:ruler}
\paragraph{What we did} We first wanted to change \emph{only} the location of the artifact. We took 2,437 dermoscopy
images with manual lesion masks (ISIC 2018 \citep{codella2019isic}; task: melanoma or not) and pasted the same small
ruler into each image at 0, 25, 50, 75 or 100\% overlap with the lesion (Figure~\ref{fig:ruler}). Everything else stays
the same: same images, same ruler, same labels.
\begin{figure}[!htb]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/ruler_examples.pdf}
\caption{The same image with the ruler at 0, 50 and 100\% overlap with the lesion (green: lesion mask). The pasted ruler is smaller than most real rulers so that it can lie fully inside the lesion; inside the lesion it covers about the same share of the lesion's area as real hair does (3.0\% vs 3.3\%).}
\label{fig:ruler}\end{figure}

\paragraph{What we found} When the ruler lies outside the lesion, masking removes it and helps a lot (+0.18). As soon
as the ruler overlaps the lesion, masking \emph{hurts}: at full overlap the masked model is worse than the standard
model ($-0.13$; Table~\ref{tab:ruler}, Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}). The clearest sign is on the correlated test: with the
ruler on the lesion, the masked model scores \emph{higher} than the standard model there (0.961 vs 0.937) but much
lower on the reversed test (0.386 vs 0.515). It has become \emph{more} dependent on the ruler.

\paragraph{Is it a fluke of one model or one ruler?} No. We repeated the experiment with a smaller image size, with a
dermatology-specific encoder (DermLIP \citep{yan2025derm1m}), and with a ruler whose colour, ticks and size change from
image to image. The pattern was the same every time (Table~\ref{tab:rep}). We summarise it with one number, the
\emph{location effect}: the benefit of masking with the ruler outside minus its benefit with the ruler fully inside.

\section{Step 2: why does masking fail?}
Step~1 showed \emph{that} masking fails when the ruler is inside. There were three possible reasons:
(a) masking simply hides useful parts of the image; (b) the background contains disease information that masking
throws away; (c) the part of the ruler inside the lesion survives, and because masking deletes everything that
competed with it, the model relies on it more. We designed one check for each (Table~\ref{tab:why}).

\begin{table}[!htb]\centering\small
\caption{Four checks of why masking fails (ruler experiment, DINOv2).}\label{tab:why}
\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\begin{tabular}{P{3.3cm}P{4.6cm}P{4.4cm}P{3.6cm}}\toprule
Check & What we changed & Result & What it means \\\midrule
1. Useless ruler & Ruler at the same places, but \emph{unrelated} to the diagnosis & Masking does not hurt at any position (all intervals include 0; e.g.\ $+0.039$ at 100\%) & Hiding parts of the image is not the problem (rules out a) \\
2. Bigger mask & Grow the mask around the lesion so that more of a half-covered ruler stays visible (50\% $\to$ 95\%) & Harm gets \emph{worse}: $-0.097 \to -0.153$; when the ruler is already fully visible, a bigger mask changes nothing ($-0.129$ vs $-0.138$) & Harm follows how much of the ruler is visible, not how much context is lost (rules out b) \\
3. Same-image test & Add or remove the ruler on the \emph{same} test image and see how much the prediction changes & Masked model's prediction changes about 2 times as much as the standard model's (0.49 vs 0.24) & The masked model leans more on the ruler (supports c) \\
4. Lesion size & Split test images into small, medium and large lesions & Harm with the ruler inside: small $-0.36$, medium $-0.16$, large $+0.06$ (not significant) & Harm is largest where the ruler is big compared to the lesion \\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}}\end{table}

\keybox{\textbf{Explanation.} Masking deletes everything outside the ROI. If the artifact is outside, the shortcut is
gone. If the artifact is inside, it survives, and the other image information that used to compete with it is gone, so
the artifact becomes a bigger part of what the model can use. The model relies on it more, and fails when the
artifact is missing or misleading.}

\section{Step 3: is it only skin images?}
\paragraph{What we did} We repeated the moving-artifact experiment in four other kinds of medical images, each with its
own typical artifact and its own ROI (Figure~\ref{fig:sweepex}): a sonographer's caliper on \textbf{thyroid
ultrasound} (TN3K, malignant or benign nodule \citep{gong2021tn3k}) and on \textbf{ovarian ultrasound} (MMOTU, tumour
with or without a solid part \citep{zhao2022mmotu}); a debris blob on \textbf{capsule endoscopy} (SEE-AI, erosion or
polyp-like lesion \citep{seeai2023}); and a tube on \textbf{chest X-rays} (NIH ChestX-ray14, pneumothorax
\citep{wang2017chestxray8}; ROI = the lungs). We used only images without a real artifact, so the pasted one was the only
one. We also tested a second encoder where possible (MedSigLIP, a medical image model; RAD-DINO, a chest X-ray model
\citep{perezgarcia2025raddino}).
\begin{figure}[!htb]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/sweep_examples.pdf}
\caption{The pasted artifact at 0, 50 and 100\% overlap in three modalities (green: ROI; for capsule images the ROI is
the expert's box around the lesion).}\label{fig:sweepex}\end{figure}

\paragraph{What we found} The location effect was positive in 10 of the 11 experiments of Steps 1 and 3
(Table~\ref{tab:other}, Figure~\ref{fig:all}). Masking helped clearly when the artifact was outside and lost most or
all of its benefit when the artifact was inside. Whether masking actually \emph{hurt} at full overlap depended on the
setting: it hurt in 7 of 11 experiments. In thyroid ultrasound, for example, the benefit dropped from +0.40 to about zero.

\paragraph{The one exception} With the chest X-ray encoder RAD-DINO, the standard model simply ignored a tube outside

so the comparison does not apply. With DINOv2, which did use the outside tube, the usual pattern appeared.

\section{Step 4: real artifacts}
Pasted artifacts let us control location exactly, but they are not the artifacts clinicians produce. So we next
asked whether the same thing happens with \emph{real, unedited} artifacts.

\paragraph{The design} A real artifact cannot be moved, so we compare two groups of images: those whose artifact lies
mostly inside the ROI (\textbf{Trap A}) and those whose artifact lies outside it (\textbf{Trap B}). Both traps share the
\emph{same} artifact-free images (Figure~\ref{fig:traps}), so the main designed difference between them is where the
artifact sits. (We first tried comparing in-ROI against out-of-ROI images directly, without an artifact-free group.
That design cannot work: masking deletes exactly the out-of-ROI artifact that defines one side of the comparison, so
it shows ``harm'' whatever the truth.) Each trap then gets the 90/10 training set and the three test sets of
Figure~\ref{fig:env}. The \textbf{crossover} is the benefit of masking in Trap~B minus its benefit in Trap~A; a positive
crossover means masking is worth more when the artifact is outside.
\begin{figure}[!htb]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/two_traps.pdf}
\caption{Two traps built from real images. Both share one group of artifact-free images.}\label{fig:traps}\end{figure}

\paragraph{Cohorts} Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}. Artifacts were located automatically: published hair masks for skin
images \citep{wegley2026artifacts}, a rule-based caliper detector for ultrasound, and a small detector trained on expert
debris masks for capsule images. We checked them by eye: the caliper detector was right on 38 of 40 checked images.
Detection errors cannot create a crossover: they weaken the shortcut in both traps equally.

\paragraph{How to read it} For skin hair, masking actually \emph{hurt} when the hair lay on the lesion. For calipers and
debris, masking still helped a little in Trap~A, because it removes other on-screen marks and clutter, but a large part
of the shortcut stayed. In thyroid Trap~A, the masked model still scored 0.908 on the correlated test but only 0.398 on
the reversed one; in Trap~B masking closed this gap (0.785 vs 0.752). The general rule is therefore: \emph{masking is worth
much less when the artifact is inside the ROI}. Whether it actually hurts depends on how much useful information
masking also removes.

\section{Other things we learned}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Methods that know where the artifact is fix both traps.} Reweighting the training images so that the
artifact no longer predicts the label, or retraining the last layer on balanced data (DFR \citep{kirichenko2023dfr}),
helped in both traps of every cohort, wherever the artifact was (Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The catch is that they need an
artifact label for every training image, which clinical datasets rarely have; masking needs only the ROI.
\item \textbf{Painting out the artifact can backfire.} In capsule images, painting over the detected debris
\emph{lowered} the score ($-0.17$ in Trap~A), because erosions contain yellow-white material that looks like debris.
Deleting artifact pixels is only safe when the artifact does not look like the disease.
\item \textbf{The hair shortcut is not only in the hair pixels.} Hair goes together with patient age, sex and body
site. Painting out the hair pixels closed less than half of the standard model's shortcut gap (47\% in Trap~A, 44\% in
Trap~B); the rest comes from things that go with hair. Masking cannot remove such a shortcut.
\item \textbf{One encoder did not follow the rule.} With DermLIP, masking hurt in \emph{both} hair traps and there was
no crossover: this encoder needs the skin around the lesion, so any mask costs disease information.
\item \textbf{Chest X-rays are a boundary case.} Real chest drains lie inside the lungs and are an extreme shortcut
(standard model: reversed AUROC 0.19 against 0.89 on the clean test); lung masking barely helped (0.23), while DFR
reached 0.67. With real devices (catheters in the lungs vs breathing tubes outside), RAD-DINO showed no crossover, while
MedSigLIP showed a positive one in all four findings tested. The device types also differ in which other devices are
present, so we do not count chest X-rays as a confirmation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Questions for discussion}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Is the reversed-test AUROC acceptable as the main score, or should a clinical measure (sensitivity at a fixed
