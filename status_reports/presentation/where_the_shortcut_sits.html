Where the Shortcut Sits

Does masking the region of interest remove artifact shortcuts from medical image classifiers? It depends on where the artifact sits.

Masking helps when the artifact is outside the region of interest. When the artifact is inside, masking loses its benefit and often makes the shortcut worse.

Move with the arrow keys, the buttons below, or by clicking the ruler at the bottom. Most slides are interactive: click the papers, flip the toggles, drag the slider.

The problem

Classifiers learn the ruler, not the disease

A deep classifier learns whatever predicts the label. In skin images, doctors often place a ruler next to a suspicious mole, so a model can score well by detecting the ruler. This is a shortcut.

A common fix is ROI masking: keep only the region of interest (ROI), such as the lesion, and replace the rest of the image.

Its hidden assumption: the artifact lies in the background. Rulers, hair, calipers and debris often lie on the lesion too.

Our 90/10 training and 10/90 reversed test apply the same idea to medical artifacts, scored with AUROC, together with correlated and clean tests on the same images.

What is new in our design

Earlier work did the parts, not the combination

Reversed tests and moving a cue have been done before. What we did not find is the full combination: the same artifact placed inside versus outside the ROI, crossed with ROI masking, and each condition tested on correlated, reversed and clean tests.

Closest precedents: Bassi et al. (matched, reversed and clean tests; the mark is always outside the lungs), Puli et al. (90/10 → 10/90 on chest X-rays), Bissoto et al. (reversed skin artifacts, with masking; workshop), Corbetta et al. (inverted artifacts, MICCAI 2026), Stanley et al. and Boland et al. (bias near vs far, or fixed vs random position, not relative to a masking ROI). Li et al. (CVPR 2023) showed in natural images that removing one shortcut can amplify another.

Datasets we used

Six public datasets, four kinds of medical images

How we test it

Two models, one trap

Both models use the same frozen image encoder (mainly DINOv2) and the same simple classifier. Only the input differs: the standard model sees the whole image, the masked model sees only the ROI.

To measure reliance on the artifact we build a trap: in training, the artifact is on 90% of disease images and 10% of the others.

The score

AUROC on the reversed test, where the artifact–label link is flipped (0.5 = guessing, 1.0 = perfect). We report masked minus standard: positive means masking helped.

The location effect is that difference with the artifact outside the ROI minus the difference with it fully inside.

Why AUROC?

We ask “does the model rank sick above healthy?”, and AUROC measures exactly that

AUROC = pick one sick and one healthy image at random: how often does the model give the sick one the higher score? 1.0 = always, 0.5 = coin flip, below 0.5 = it gets the order backwards.

Toy example with 10 images on the reversed test: the artifact is now mostly on healthy images. A ring marks an image with the artifact.

Step 1 · is it a fluke?

The location effect survives every change we tried

We repeated the ruler experiment at a lower resolution, with a dermatology encoder (DermLIP), with a ruler whose colour, ticks and size change from image to image, and with a long thin ruler like real scale bars.

The location effect stayed clearly above zero every time. Click a row to see what masking did at each end.

Step 2 · why does masking fail?

The ruler survives, its competitors are deleted

Three possible reasons: (a) masking simply hides useful parts of the image; (b) the background holds disease information; (c) the ruler inside the lesion survives, and because masking deletes what competed with it, the model relies on it more.

Four checks point to (c).

Same image, ruler added

How much the predicted probability (0 to 1) changes when the ruler is added to the same test image. Larger = the model leans more on the ruler.

Step 3 · is it only skin images?

The same pattern in ultrasound, capsule endoscopy and chest X-rays

We moved a typical artifact into the ROI in four kinds of medical images: a caliper in thyroid and ovarian ultrasound, debris in capsule endoscopy, a tube in chest X-rays.

The location effect was positive in 10 of 11 experiments. The exception is RAD-DINO, a chest X-ray encoder that ignored a tube outside the lungs, so masking had nothing to remove.

How to read this chart

  • Each row (y axis) is one dataset and one image model, e.g. “Thyroid, calipers · DINOv2”.
  • The x axis is how much masking changed the model’s score on the hard test (masked model’s AUROC minus normal model’s AUROC). Right of 0 = masking helped; left of 0 = masking made it worse.
  • Orange bar: images with the artifact on the lesion (inside the ROI).
    Teal bar: images with the artifact away from the lesion (outside the ROI).
  • A long teal bar and a short orange bar means masking works well only when the artifact is away from the lesion. That is our finding.
  • The number on the right = teal minus orange: how much more masking helps when the artifact is away. Large = location matters a lot; near 0 = location does not matter (DermLIP). “Clear” = its 95% interval stays above 0.

A future option · second chest X-ray hospital

PadChest could test the chest X-ray result at another hospital

About 160,000 chest X-rays from about 67,000 patients (San Juan Hospital, Spain, 2009–2017). It would give a second device trap: drains or catheters inside the lungs (Trap A) against endotracheal or tracheostomy tubes outside them (Trap B).

What it has

  • Device labels from the reports: endotracheal tube, nasogastric tube, chest drain, central venous catheter.
  • NEATX tube labels (about 1,000; chest drain, tracheostomy, nasogastric and endotracheal tubes), the same source as our NIH drain labels.
  • Patient IDs, so we can keep each patient on one side of the split.
  • Lung masks through CheXmask, for the ROI.

What it lacks

  • No pixel masks of the devices: labels say a tube is present, not where. Location comes from the device type, as in our NIH device traps.
  • Noisy labels: 73% come from a text model reading Spanish reports; 27% were labelled by physicians.
  • Few NEATX labels per tube type: probably too few for our minimum trap sizes on their own.
  • Heavy access: about 1 TB, research-use agreement with BIMCV, no redistribution of images.

Worth doing only if chest X-rays stay in the main text. Cheap first step: request access and count the device groups from the label file before downloading any images.

Questions for discussion

Where we would like your view

  1. 1. The main score

    Is the reversed-test AUROC acceptable as the main score, or should a clinical measure (sensitivity at a fixed specificity) be added from the start?

  2. 2. How to state the rule

    “Masking is worth less inside the ROI” (true in all real cohorts) or “masking hurts inside the ROI” (true for skin hair/ruler artifacts and in 7 of 11 moving-artifact experiments)?

  3. 3. The artifact masks

    The masks were checked by eye by a non-clinician. Should a clinician check a sample?

  4. 4. Chest X-rays

    Present them as a boundary case, or leave them out of the main text? If they stay, should we add PadChest as a second hospital?
