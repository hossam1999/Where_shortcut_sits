Where the Shortcut Sits

Does masking the region of interest remove artifact shortcuts from medical image classifiers? It depends on where the artifact sits.

Masking helps when the artifact is outside the region of interest. When the artifact is inside, masking loses its benefit and often makes the shortcut worse.

Use the arrow keys, the buttons below, or click the ruler to move between slides. Several slides are interactive: try the toggles and the slider.

The problem

Classifiers learn the ruler, not the disease

A deep classifier learns whatever predicts the label. In skin images, doctors often place a ruler next to a suspicious mole. A model can then score well by detecting the ruler. This is a shortcut.

A common fix is ROI masking: keep only the region of interest (ROI), such as the lesion, and replace the rest of the image.

Its hidden assumption: the artifact lies in the background. Rulers, hair, calipers and debris often lie on the lesion too.

Press the button. Teal line = ROI, orange = the artifact.

How we test it

Two models, one trap

Both models use the same frozen image encoder (mainly DINOv2) and the same simple classifier. Only the input differs: the standard model sees the whole image, the masked model sees only the ROI.

To measure reliance on the artifact we build a trap. In training the artifact appears on 90% of the disease images and 10% of the others, so it is a strong shortcut.

The score

AUROC on the reversed test, where the artifact–label link is flipped. 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect. We report masked minus standard: positive means masking helped.

The location effect is that difference with the artifact outside the ROI minus the difference with it fully inside.

Step 1 · is it a fluke?

The location effect survives every change we tried

We repeated the ruler experiment at a lower resolution, with a dermatology encoder (DermLIP), with a ruler whose colour, ticks and size change from image to image, and with a long thin ruler like real scale bars.

The location effect stayed large and positive every time. Click a bar to see what masking did at each end.

Location effect with its 95% interval (reversed test). Right of zero = masking is worth more when the ruler is outside.

Step 2 · why does masking fail?

The ruler survives, its competitors are deleted

Three possible reasons: (a) masking simply hides useful parts of the image; (b) the background holds disease information; (c) the ruler inside the lesion survives, and because masking deletes everything that competed with it, the model relies on it more.

Four checks point to (c).

Step 3 · is it only skin images?

The same pattern in ultrasound, capsule endoscopy and chest X-rays

We moved a typical artifact into the ROI in four kinds of medical images: a caliper in thyroid and ovarian ultrasound, debris in capsule endoscopy, a tube in chest X-rays.

The location effect was positive in 10 of 11 experiments. The one exception is RAD-DINO, a chest X-ray encoder that ignored a tube outside the lungs, so there was nothing for masking to remove.

Step 4 · real artifacts

Real artifacts behave the same way

A real artifact cannot be moved. So we build two traps from the same artifact-free images: Trap A uses images with the artifact inside the ROI, Trap B images with it outside.

The crossover is masking's benefit in Trap B minus its benefit in Trap A. Positive = masking helps more when the artifact is outside.

Positive in all four cohorts with DINOv2 (the four planned tests, all significant after correction), and in every cohort tested with MedSigLIP and ConvNeXt. Not with DermLIP.

Other things we learned

Methods that know the artifact help in both traps, but need labels

  • Reweighting and DFR helped in both traps of every cohort, wherever the artifact lay. The catch: they need an artifact label for training images, which clinical datasets rarely have. Masking needs only the ROI.
  • Painting out the artifact can backfire. In capsule images it lowered the score (−0.17 in Trap A), likely because erosions contain material that looks like debris.
  • DermLIP did not follow the rule. It seems to need the skin around the lesion: masking lowered its clean-test score too.
  • Chest X-rays are a boundary case. The device groups also differ in other devices present, so we do not count them as a confirmation.

Questions for discussion

Where we would like your view

  1. 1. The main score

    Is the reversed-test AUROC acceptable as the main score, or should a clinical measure (sensitivity at a fixed specificity) be added from the start?

  2. 2. How to state the rule

    “Masking is worth less inside the ROI” (true in all real cohorts) or “masking hurts inside the ROI” (true for skin hair/ruler artifacts and in 7 of 11 moving-artifact experiments)?

  3. 3. The artifact masks

    The masks were checked by eye by a non-clinician. Should a clinician check a sample?

  4. 4. Chest X-rays

    Present them as a boundary case, or leave them out of the main text?
