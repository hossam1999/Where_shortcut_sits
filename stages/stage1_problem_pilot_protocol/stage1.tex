\stagebox{\textbf{In one sentence.} Region-of-interest (ROI) masking is the standard defence against acquisition
artifacts that act as shortcuts; this project asks whether it still works when the artifact lies \emph{inside} the ROI.
This first meeting presents the clinical problem, the pilot that raised the question, its independent replication, and
the protocol that every later stage uses --- including a correction to the bootstrap that widened every interval.}

\section{Where we are}
This is the first of six meetings. It sets up everything the later stages rely on: the question, the evidence from
the pilot study (experiments E1--E15), an independent re-implementation of that pilot from its written specification,
and the experimental protocol --- environments, frozen backbones, linear heads, grouped splits, pre-registration and
the statistical estimator. What is new relative to the pilot is (i) that every pilot claim was re-derived from scratch
and compared number by number, and (ii) that the uncertainty estimator was corrected after a flaw was found late in
the project; all intervals in these six reports use the corrected estimator.

\section{Question}
\subsection{Why shortcut artifacts matter clinically}
Deep classifiers exploit features that are correlated with the diagnosis in the training data but are not evidence of
disease \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,degrave2021ai}. In dermoscopy, surgical skin markings \citep{winkler2019markings}
and ruler scale bars \citep{winkler2021scalebars} changed the melanoma predictions of a market-approved system; in
chest radiography, pneumothorax classifiers detect the chest drain that treats the condition
\citep{oakden2020hidden,jimenez2023shortcuts}. Such correlations arise from clinical workflow --- suspicious lesions are
measured and marked, sonographers place calipers on nodules they consider suspicious --- so they are present in every
retrospectively collected dataset, and a model that relies on them fails exactly on the patients whose workflow
differs (for example, a malignant nodule that was not measured).

\subsection{The research question and why we asked it this way}
The most widely recommended defence is to restrict the classifier to the ROI: segment the lesion and discard the
background. Its rationale assumes that the shortcut lives in the background. Rulers, hair and calipers do not respect
that assumption; when an artifact overlaps the ROI, masking cannot remove it and removes everything \emph{else}. The
question is therefore:
\begin{quote}\emph{Does the benefit of ROI masking depend on where the artifact sits relative to the ROI --- and if so,
which quantity governs that dependence, and does it matter on unaltered clinical data?}\end{quote}
\paragraph{Design decisions and rejected alternatives}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]\itemsep1pt
\item \emph{Control the overlap first, then use real artifacts.} The pilot pasted a synthetic ruler at controlled
overlap with the lesion (0--100\%). This isolates location from everything else; real artifacts (Stage~3) come later
because their location is confounded with the images that carry them (Stage~4). Starting with real artifacts only was
rejected because a location effect could not be separated from population differences.
\item \emph{Frozen foundation-model features and linear heads.} This is how foundation models are typically deployed on
small medical datasets, it makes every arm comparable (same features, only the head or the input changes), and it is
cheap enough to repeat over seeds, overlaps and cohorts. End-to-end fine-tuning was kept as a replication check
(Stage~5), not the main protocol, because it multiplies compute by two orders of magnitude.
\item \emph{A trap design with a reversed test.} Training with a strong artifact--label association and testing on the
reversed association measures shortcut reliance directly; clean and correlated tests measure the cost of a
mitigation. A single natural test set was rejected as the main design because its shortcut is too weak to measure
location effects precisely; natural test sets are used in Stage~5.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methods}
\paragraph{Pilot cohort} ISIC 2018 Task 1/2 images with manual lesion masks and melanoma labels from the union of the
ISIC 2016 and 2017 tables (three images with conflicting labels dropped); perceptual-hash (Hamming $\le8$) and lesion
grouping; \sOneNPilot{} images for which a $65\times19$\,px ruler can be placed at 0/25/50/75/100\% overlap within 10
percentage points \citep{codella2019isic}. Placements are frozen and reused by every run.
\paragraph{Backbones and heads} DINOv2 ViT-B/14 at 518\,px \citep{oquab2024dinov2} (CLS token), DINOv2 at 224\,px and
the dermatology foundation model DermLIP at 224\,px \citep{yan2025derm1m}; logistic-regression heads; regularisation
and decision threshold (maximal balanced accuracy) chosen on clean validation data only.
\paragraph{Environments} Training and correlated test use $P(A{=}1\mid Y{=}1)=0.9$ and $P(A{=}1\mid Y{=}0)=0.1$; the
reversed test swaps them; the clean test has no artifact (sweeps) or an artifact independent of the label (real traps).
Figure~\ref{fig:env} shows the four environments. Seeds: 42, 123, 456 for the sweeps.

\subsection{The corrected bootstrap}\label{sec:bootstrap}
Every comparison is a paired difference of AUROCs between two arms on the same test images, averaged over training
seeds. The original estimator (inherited from the pilot) resampled seeds and then, \emph{independently within each
sampled seed}, the test images. In most analyses, however, every seed scores the \emph{same} test images (a fixed test
split, or cross-validation folds that together cover the same pool). Resampling images independently per seed then
treats one test set as several and divides the test-sampling variance by the number of seeds: intervals were too
narrow. The corrected (\emph{crossed}) estimator resamples seeds with replacement and draws one Poisson(1) weight per
test image, shared by every seed in which the image appears, then recomputes the per-seed statistic and averages
(\texttt{wtss.stats\_crossed}; 10,000 replicates). Every interval in these reports comes from regenerated runs with this
estimator (Sec.~\ref{sec:correction} gives its consequences).

\subsection{Pre-registration}
Before every experiment a document stating the design, the claims and what would count as support was committed to
the repository; results were committed afterwards. The registry lists \sOneNRegistered{} registrations; every one
precedes its first outcome result except that the SLAS registration and its localisation result share a commit, and
the 16-test primary family (Stage~3) was grouped after the individual results. \textbf{Caveat:} commit times come from
the machine that made the commits and are not independent proof of order; only the registrations of the last three
rounds were additionally pushed to GitHub before running, which provides an external time stamp.

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Replication} The original ledger (before the bootstrap correction) had \sOneOrigMatch{} MATCH,
\sOneOrigSignCI{} SIGN+CI and \sOneOrigMismatch{} MISMATCH among \sOneOrigN{} numbers. Re-checked with the regenerated
runs and the corrected estimator, the counts are \sOneNewMatch{} MATCH, \sOneNewSignCI{} SIGN+CI and \sOneNewMismatch{}

The central pilot result replicates: in the controlled sweep, masking changes reversed-test AUROC by \sOneEOneZero{} at
0\% overlap and by \sOneEOneHundred{} at 100\% overlap (location interaction \sOneEOneInter).

\paragraph{The mismatches} With the corrected estimator the ledger has \sOneNewMismatch{} mismatches (originally
\sOneOrigMismatch). (i) E1, masking at 25\% overlap: the pilot's interval excluded zero, the corrected one includes it

\section{What failed or changed}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Bootstrap flaw (found in the third review round).} The inherited estimator understated uncertainty
whenever seeds share test images. All runs were regenerated and every interval recomputed; claims that lost support are
listed in Stage~4.
\item \textbf{Three pilot mismatches}, above; the E12 design failure is discussed in Stage~3.
\item \textbf{Rebuild on new hardware.} All data were re-downloaded and every derived object rebuilt on a different GPU;
the lesion U-Net was retrained, which changed hair-trap membership by about 3\%. Two gaps in the reproduction recipe
(\texttt{Makefile}) were found and fixed.
\item \textbf{Commit times are not independent proof} of pre-registration order (above).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Shortcut artifacts are a clinical safety problem, and ROI masking --- the usual defence --- rests on an untested
assumption about where the shortcut lives. The pilot suggested that masking reverses from helpful to harmful as the
artifact moves into the lesion; an independent re-implementation reproduced it. The protocol --- trap environments,
frozen foundation-model features, grouped splits, pre-registration and a crossed bootstrap --- is fixed here and used
unchanged in every later stage. \textbf{Supported:} the pilot's central sweep result and most of its numbers
replicate. \textbf{Not supported:} the pilot's null overlap-contrast result (E12); the original, narrower intervals.

\section{Questions for the supervisor}

any study that averages several training seeds on one test set)?

\end{enumerate}
