\section{Mechanism: masking retains an in-ROI artifact and removes its competitors}\label{sec:mech}
Three explanations were tested in the controlled ruler sweep. \emph{Occlusion} alone does not harm: with the ruler
present but uncorrelated with the label, masking changes AUROC by $+0.036$ to $+0.041$ at every overlap, none
significantly, so the harm requires the correlation. \emph{Lost context} is not the driver: widening the mask so that
it restores ruler pixels increases the harm. \emph{Amplified reliance} is: with the head held fixed, inserting the ruler
on the lesion changes the masked model's prediction about twice as much as ERM's ($|\Delta p|$ 0.491 versus 0.235 at
full overlap; paired difference $+0.255$ [$+0.221, +0.287$]), because masking removes the competing evidence around it.
The same holds for ovarian calipers ($+0.188$ [$+0.133, +0.250$]) and capsule debris ($+0.186$ [$+0.130, +0.260$]);
for thyroid calipers the masked model relies on the caliper as much as ERM does (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}). Harm is
largest for small lesions, where the ruler is a larger share of what survives the mask (Supplement~S14).

For real hair, removing every hair pixel at test time closes only 44--47\% of ERM's clean--reversed gap: most of what a
frozen encoder exploits is what accompanies hair (age, sex, body site), not the hair itself. This bounds every
pixel-level method and explains why methods that use image-level artifact labels, which remove everything correlated
with the label, recover more. It also limits the transplant: a pasted hair carries the shortcut in its pixels by
construction, so the transplant shows the location effect for pixel-carried hair, not its size for hair in the wild.
